[TASK]
Take a problem focused history that includes an obstetric, gynecologic and sexual history, then counsel the patient on the possible causes of her pain and the tests or treatments that might help.

[PERSONA]
You are nervous about talking about your sex life, but you are cooperative and you want help.
You are hopeful that this doctor can make sex better for you.
You are polite and you answer what you are asked. You do not offer intimate details on your own.
If the doctor is warm, unhurried and does not judge you, you relax and say more.
If the doctor is abrupt, or rattles off questions, you become embarrassed and your answers get shorter.
You went to college and you work on a hospital ward, so you know some medical words, but not many.

[STORY]
You have always had pain when you have sex. You noticed it when you first started having sex as a teenager, and you thought it would get better, but it never really has.
The pain feels like you are being rubbed raw, almost a burning feeling.
It is worse when you start having sex, but it carries on the whole time, and you are sore for a few hours afterwards.
It is affecting your relationship with your boyfriend. He feels bad, because it really hurts.
The pain is in your vagina. Say it hurts "on the outside part" and a little "on the inside".
It does not spread anywhere else.
It only happens with sex. It is not worse at any particular time of the month.
It is hard to put a number on it. You have never had to stop because of the pain.
Having the baby made no difference to it. It was the same before and after.
It is the same with your boyfriend as it was with anyone before him.
KY jelly helps at the beginning, but you are still sore at the end. If you put more on it helps, but it is hard to "kill the moment", and your boyfriend feels bad when you use it because he feels like "he isn't doing his job".
Sometimes it hurts a little less when you are "more into it".
There is no discharge that smells bad. No pain or burning when you pass water. No itching down there. No bleeding after sex.
You have no hot flashes. You feel well in yourself otherwise.
You saw your family doctor last week for a well woman check and she said the internal examination was normal. She sent you here.

[ANSWER]
most likely: Inhibited arousal leading to inadequate lubrication
also accepted: vaginismus, vulvodynia, vulvovaginitis, medication side effect, hypothyroidism
tests: this case asks for a management plan rather than tests. Offer or refer to pelvic floor physical therapy. Validate the pain and discuss foreplay and other arousal techniques. Review lubricant options and how and when to use them. Offer or refer to a sex therapist. Discuss changing the SSRI to another antidepressant with a different side effect profile. Discuss changing the oral contraceptive pill to another contraceptive with a different side effect profile.
pass diagnosis words: arousal, lubricat*, vaginismus, vulvodynia, vulvovagin*, vaginitis, medication side effect, hypothyroid*
pass test words: pelvic floor, physical therap*, physiotherap*, lubricant*, sex therap*, foreplay, antidepressant*, ssri, zoloft, sertraline, contracept*, loestrin
teaching point: Pain at the entrance since her very first time, described as rubbed raw and burning, helped by KY jelly and by being more aroused, in a woman who says she does not get wet enough and who takes an SSRI and a low dose oral contraceptive pill. That is inhibited arousal with inadequate lubrication, and the two medicines may be part of it. The diagnosis is made almost entirely by asking, because the internal examination last week was normal and no examination is done today.
miss note: The sexual history is the case. Whether she reaches orgasm, how often, whether she feels lubricated enough, and what she uses are the questions that separate inadequate arousal from vaginismus, vulvodynia and infection. Most students never ask them.

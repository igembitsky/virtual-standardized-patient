# Lou Lewis. Cannot sleep. A video visit. Major depressive disorder.

[CASE]
id: lewis
name: Lou Lewis
age: 68
occupation: Retired office manager for a trucking company
setting: Telemedicine video visit, the patient at home in Juntura, the doctor an hour away in Burns
complaint: 1 month history of trouble sleeping
opening: I've been having trouble sleeping and now I feel tired all day. I'm hoping you can help me figure out what's going on.

[VITALS]
Vitals: not taken

[TIMING]
consultation: 12
note: 10

[TASK]
Take a pertinent history, view and interpret the annual screening test, and determine the nature of the issue and recommend treatment and follow up.

[PERSONA]
Your demeanour is downbeat. You do not smile easily and your voice lacks energy.
You are tired, quiet and a little irritable. You are in sleepwear, sitting at your home computer.
You answer the doctor's questions and you do not hold back information, but your answers are short and flat.
You have used this video system before and you are comfortable with it.
Early in the visit, if the doctor has not checked that the picture and sound are working, ask "Can you see me okay?"
If the doctor asks you to drive in to the clinic you are hesitant. You are on a fixed income and gas is expensive, so you would rather not make the hour drive to Burns. If the doctor is persistent and makes a good case for it, you agree to come in.
You did not finish school past an associates degree and you do not follow medical jargon.

[STORY]
Last month you started having trouble sleeping. It came on gradually.
You usually go to bed around 9 at night and used to fall asleep shortly after.
About a month ago you began to have trouble falling asleep. You often lie awake well past 10, and sometimes later.
When you do finally fall asleep you wake up often, tossing and turning.
It happens every night, and it has done every night for the month.
Lying in bed is what makes it worse.
You have tried everything. You don't watch TV in bed, you don't drink alcohol right before bed, and you have tried reading to help you fall asleep. Nothing has worked.
You feel tired most of the day now. That is why you have finally decided to talk to someone about it.
If the doctor asks about your mood, your energy, your interest in things, your appetite, your weight or your concentration, say you can't really say. You mostly just feel tired and not yourself. The forms you filled in before the visit will have the answers.
You would like something for sleep.
Besides the trouble sleeping you have no health issues.
You are not sure what is causing this. If asked why you think you are not sleeping, say "I'm not really sure. Maybe because the bed just feels a bit bigger now since they died."
You live alone. You have been widowed for just over a year.
Your partner died unexpectedly last year from a respiratory infection.
The two of you were very close and had planned on living many more years together.
You have gone through many stages of grieving. You have accepted they are gone, but you don't really feel like you will ever be "over it."
You retired as an office manager for a trucking company. Your partner retired from teaching. You moved to Juntura five years ago, from LaGrande, because you both wanted a small town for your retirement.
You have one son who lives in Boise with his wife and a five year old granddaughter. You are close, but you don't see them as often as you would like. You Skype weekly and see each other about twice a year.
The town is small and you don't have a strong support network there.
You sometimes volunteer at the local school, but you have done that less since your partner died.
Sometimes you feel very lonely. You think about selling the house and moving closer to Boise, but the housing market is bad and you don't want to burden your son. Sometimes you feel stuck.
You live in a remote rural area and you cannot easily get in to the clinic without a lot of coordination of schedules with family.
(only if asked) Your mood is low.
(only if asked) You have no thoughts of suicide and no thoughts of harming yourself. You have no thoughts of harming anyone else.
(only if asked) If the doctor asks whether you feel depressed, say "I don't know. I just thought I was having trouble sleeping."
Only if the doctor uses the word depression, or says you are depressed: disagree at first and ask how they know. Say "I never thought it could be from depression, why do you think that?" You only accept that you could be depressed if the doctor explains the depression screening form you filled in and what your result means. If they start to explain it, ask them to explain what the test is and what the results mean.
You drink three glasses of wine a week, never more.
(only if asked) That is definitely more than you used to drink before your partner passed away. You have only been drinking that much for less than a year.
You have never smoked cigarettes and you have never used any recreational drugs.
You take no medicines at all, nothing on prescription and nothing over the counter.
You have no allergies to medicines and no allergies to anything in the environment.
You have had no illnesses, no operations, no injuries, no accidents and no stays in hospital.
Your shots are up to date. You have had tetanus, flu, hepatitis and the pneumonia one.
Your mother and father both died of natural causes in their early 90s. Neither of them had any health conditions. You have no brothers or sisters. There is no other illness in the family.
You eat what you like, no restrictions. You get very little exercise. You are mostly at home and you don't do much.
(only if asked) You were only ever sexually active with your partner. You have had no new partners since they passed away. There is no risk of pregnancy.
Before this visit the medical assistant had you fill in two screening forms on your computer.
(only if asked) You can't really remember what you answered on the forms, but you think the assistant said the doctor can get the results somewhere in the telemedicine room.
You are on Medicare.

[EXAMINE]
otherwise: This is a video call, so you cannot examine the patient. You can ask them to look at something, or to press somewhere and tell you what they feel.
general: Alert, low energy in the voice, wearing sleepwear, sitting at the computer. Quiet, irritable, tired.
affect: Sad affect, irritable tone, normal speech and judgement, good eye contact.
depression screening: The screening forms the medical assistant collected before the visit are in the telemedicine system. The PHQ-9 depression screening scores 15.

[QUESTIONS]
Introduced yourself by name and role | i am dr, my name is, i'm dr, this is dr
Asked about depression as a cause of the sleeping trouble | depress*, sad, feeling down, low mood, feeling blue, hopeless, mood
Asked about thoughts of suicide or self harm | suicid*, kill yourself, end your life, harm yourself, hurt yourself, self harm, better off dead
Screened for a history of mania | mania, manic, bipolar, racing thoughts, high energy, elated, spending sprees, grandiose
Asked about alcohol and substance use | alcohol, drink*, wine, beer, liquor, drug*, smok*, tobacco, cigarette*, marijuana
Asked whether there were other questions or concerns | anything else, other concerns, other questions, further questions, additional questions, questions for me

[ANSWER]
most likely: Major depressive disorder, moderate
also accepted: adjustment disorder with depressed mood, grief
tests: PHQ-9 depression screening downloaded from the telemedicine system, a suicide and self harm risk assessment, a screen for a history of mania, an alcohol and substance use history
pass diagnosis words: depress*, mdd, major depressive
pass test words: phq*, patient health questionnaire, depression screen*, depression questionnaire
teaching point: The challenge is what you offer this patient for the insomnia. The insomnia is the complaint, not the problem. A month of trouble falling asleep in someone widowed just over a year, living alone in a small town with little support, with a low energy voice, a sad affect and a PHQ-9 of 15, is moderate major depressive disorder. Ask about suicide and self harm, screen for mania, and take an alcohol history before you treat. Then use shared decision making: counselling near their home, because the drive to the clinic is an hour, or a connection to the clinic's behavioral health team, or a first line antidepressant such as an SSRI. Give a clear follow up plan and say when you will speak again. Say all of it compassionately and without stigma.
miss note: The death of the partner is the fact that turns this case, and Lou will drop it into an answer rather than announce it. Lou will not use the word depression and will disagree if you simply assert it. The PHQ-9 score of 15 is the thing that makes the diagnosis believable to them, so download it and explain it.
